Did you beat the market, or did the market just go up?

Enter your trades and this page runs the statistical tests a fund analyst would: it compares every trade with its own industry over the exact same dates, checks whether your results could be chance, measures what your portfolio is really made of, and stress-tests it against past crashes.

    How the numbers are calculated

    Industry proxies. Each stock is matched to one of Kenneth French's 12 industry portfolios, which have free monthly returns (including dividends) back to 1926. Index funds use the whole US market. Bonds, gold, crypto and cash have no proxy and are left out of the exposure tests.

    The luck test. For every trade with a known end price, your return is compared with its industry's return over the exact same dates. If you had no stock-picking skill, beating or trailing the industry by a given amount would be equally likely. The test flips each trade's result to a win or a loss at random 10,000 times and counts how often a no-skill investor would match your average. Each trade counts equally, whatever its size.

    Trades needed. The number of trades, at your current average edge and spread, that a one-sided test at the 5% level would need before ruling out luck.

    Your return. A money-weighted annual return (XIRR) from your actual purchase and sale dates, compared with putting exactly the same money into the whole market on the same dates. Your figures use the price change only, so dividends you received are not counted; the industry and market figures include dividends, which slightly favours them.

    Selling habits. Compares how long you held sold winners with sold losers, using a permutation test. Holding losers much longer is the disposition effect, one of the best-documented costly habits of individual investors.

    Exposure. Your current mix's monthly returns over the last ten years are regressed on the Fama-French market, size and value factors plus momentum. With industry proxies, this describes your mix's tilts, not your stock-picking.

    Your job. Correlation between your mix and your employer's industry over 20 years, compared with what a plain index fund would show, plus how your mix did in your industry's worst 10% of months.
